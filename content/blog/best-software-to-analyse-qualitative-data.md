You have transcripts, field notes, and open-ended survey responses piling up, and a decision to make before you can start coding: which software will you actually use? The options range from an expensive institutional licence to a free tab in a spreadsheet, and the "best" one depends less on features than on your sample size, your budget, and how many people need to code alongside you.

This is a practical comparison of the software researchers actually use to analyse qualitative data, what each one is built for, and how to decide without wasting a week on trial accounts.

## What to check before you pick qualitative data analysis software

Feature lists look similar across tools. The differences that matter in practice are:

- **Price and licensing.** Some tools charge per seat per year (NVivo, ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA), some charge per project or per month (Delve, Dovetail), and some are free and open source (Taguette). Check whether your institution already has a site licence before you pay out of pocket.
- **Learning curve.** NVivo and ATLAS.ti are powerful but have a real learning curve, often a week or more to get comfortable. Lighter tools like Delve or Taguette can be usable within an hour.
- **Collaboration.** If two or more people need to code the same corpus, check whether the tool supports real-time multi-user coding or only sequential handoffs of a project file.
- **Mixed-methods support.** If you also need to run queries against quantitative or demographic data (comparing codes across age groups, for example), NVivo and MAXQDA are built for that. Lighter tools generally are not.
- **Data residency and consent.** If your ethics approval restricts where data can be stored or processed, confirm the tool's hosting location and whether it trains on your data, particularly for any AI-assisted coding features.

## The main options compared

| Tool | Best for | Pricing model | Learning curve |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **NVivo** | Large, mixed-methods projects, teams | Annual licence, institutional pricing common | Steep |
| **ATLAS.ti** | Deep qualitative coding, visual network views | Annual licence or one-time desktop licence | Steep |
| **MAXQDA** | Mixed-methods, strong for surveys + interviews | Annual licence | Moderate to steep |
| **Delve** | Solo researchers and small teams, fast onboarding | Monthly or annual subscription | Gentle |
| **Dovetail** | UX research teams, tagging and clip highlights | Per-seat subscription | Gentle |
| **Taguette** | Students and anyone on a zero budget | Free, open source | Gentle |
| **Quirkos** | Visual, intuitive coding for smaller projects | One-time or subscription licence | Gentle |

None of these is objectively "best." A solo PhD student coding forty interviews and a five-person lab running a multi-site mixed-methods study need different tools.

### NVivo and ATLAS.ti: the established choice for large or mixed-methods projects

Both are the default recommendation from most university methods courses, and for good reason: they handle large corpora, support team coding with merge and comparison features, and let you run structured queries across your codes (comparing how a theme appears across demographic groups, for instance). The tradeoff is cost and a genuine learning curve. If your institution provides a licence and your project is large or long-running, this is usually the safer bet.

### MAXQDA: strong middle ground for mixed methods

MAXQDA sits between NVivo/ATLAS.ti and the lighter tools: capable of mixed-methods work and large projects, generally considered a bit more approachable to learn, with solid support for survey data specifically.

### Delve and Dovetail: fast to learn, built for smaller teams

Delve is aimed squarely at solo researchers and small qualitative studies: minimal setup, a clean coding interface, and enough structure (codebooks, memos) to support a rigorous thematic analysis without NVivo's overhead. Dovetail is popular in UX and product research teams, built around tagging clips and quotes and turning them into shareable highlight reels for stakeholders who will never open a full transcript.

### Taguette: the free option that still does the job

Taguette is open source, free, and does the essentials well: import a document, highlight passages, tag them, export your codes. It will not run cross-tab queries or manage a hundred-interview mixed-methods study, but for a student project, a pilot study, or anyone without a budget, it is a legitimate choice, not just a stopgap.

## Do you actually need dedicated software?

For a small project, a spreadsheet with one row per coded excerpt, columns for the code, the source transcript, and a memo, can be a perfectly rigorous way to do thematic analysis. It scales badly past a dozen or so interviews and offers none of the query or team-coding features above, but it costs nothing and has no learning curve. If you are coding fewer than ten interviews solo, do not feel obliged to buy or learn a dedicated tool before you have confirmed you actually need one.

## Before you code: the software choice matters less than the transcript it starts from

Whichever tool you choose, it works on what you feed it. A transcript with missed words, misattributed speakers, or hesitations silently cleaned away will quietly distort every code built on top of it, and no amount of software sophistication fixes that upstream.
