You have your interviews recorded, your field notes written up, your focus group transcripts in hand. Now comes the harder part: making sense of it all. Learning how to analyse qualitative data is one of the core skills in social sciences, health research, education, journalism, and UX, and it's often the step researchers feel least prepared for.

This guide covers the five main methods, a practical step-by-step process, and concrete examples, so you can move from raw data to defensible findings.

## What is qualitative data analysis?

Qualitative data analysis (QDA) is the process of examining non-numerical data, interview transcripts, observation notes, documents, images, open-ended survey responses, to identify patterns, themes, and meanings.

Unlike quantitative analysis, the goal is not to count or measure. The goal is to understand: why people act as they do, how they construct meaning, what shapes their experience. It is inherently interpretive, which is why method and rigour matter.

## What are the 5 methods to analyse qualitative data?

The method you choose depends on your research question, your epistemological stance, and the type of data you have collected.

### 1. Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis (TA) is the most widely used approach. You systematically read through your data, assign codes to meaningful segments, then group related codes into themes. Braun and Clarke's six-phase framework, familiarisation, coding, theme search, review, definition, and write-up, is the standard reference. TA is flexible and works across many theoretical frameworks.

### 2. Content analysis

Content analysis works on texts (transcripts, documents, media) and can be purely qualitative or mixed. You build a coding scheme, a set of categories that map to your research questions, then code each unit of meaning. It is more structured than TA and supports comparisons across a large corpus.

### 3. Grounded theory

Developed by Glaser and Strauss, grounded theory builds explanatory theory directly from the data rather than testing existing hypotheses. Coding is iterative: open coding first, then axial coding to link categories, then selective coding to identify a core category. Theoretical sampling guides data collection until saturation.

### 4. Interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA)

IPA examines how individuals give meaning to lived experience. It works best with a small sample, typically six to ten participants, analysed in depth, one by one, before cross-case themes are identified. It is widely used in clinical psychology, nursing, and health research.

### 5. Discourse analysis

Discourse analysis focuses on language itself: word choice, framing, rhetorical strategies, and what is left unsaid. It is particularly relevant when you want to understand how power, identity or social norms are constructed through talk or text. It is common in linguistics, communication studies, and critical social science.

## How do you analyse qualitative data? A step-by-step process

Whatever method you use, the general process looks like this.

**Step 1, Prepare your data**

Gather all your material in one place: transcripts, field notes, documents. Audio and video interviews must be transcribed before you can code them. Verbatim transcription captures hesitations, interruptions and pauses that can carry analytical weight. Get everything labelled and organised.

**Step 2, Immerse yourself**

Read the full corpus without coding yet. Take free notes. Let patterns surface naturally before you impose categories. Skipping this phase often leads to premature conclusions.

**Step 3, Code**

Assign labels to meaningful segments of text. Codes can be descriptive ("mentions workload"), interpretive ("feeling invisible"), or conceptual ("institutional indifference"). You can code by hand, highlighting and annotating printed transcripts, or use dedicated software such as NVivo, Atlas.ti, MAXQDA, or the open-source Taguette.

**Step 4, Group codes into themes or categories**

Look for clusters. Which codes address the same underlying idea? Which are in tension? Which appear only at the margins? This is the core interpretive work, and it is where your analysis starts to answer your research question.

**Step 5, Review and refine**

Check each theme against the full corpus. Is it genuinely grounded in the data? Is it distinct from adjacent themes? Adjust, split, or merge as needed. A theme that only appears in one transcript is usually not a theme, it is a noteworthy exception.

**Step 6, Write and interpret**

The goal is not a list of themes: it is an argument. Cite verbatim extracts to anchor your claims. Show how themes relate to each other and to your research question. Be transparent about your analytical choices.

## Can ChatGPT analyse qualitative data?

It can help, with specific, bounded tasks. Large language models are capable of suggesting codes for short passages, summarising long transcripts, and spotting terminology patterns across a corpus.

The risk is that AI-generated analysis lacks the iterative, reflexive engagement that qualitative rigour requires. When you code data yourself, you are not just labelling: you are developing familiarity with the material, noticing contradictions, and building an interpretation that is grounded in the context of each participant. Handing that process to an AI produces output, but it does not produce analysis in the methodological sense.

A reasonable approach: use AI to generate a first round of candidate codes on a subset of your data, then critically evaluate those codes against the full corpus yourself. Do not submit AI-generated coding schemes to an ethics board or a journal without independent verification.

## What are examples of qualitative data analysis?

- **Healthcare**: semi-structured interviews with patients managing a chronic condition, analysed by IPA to map coping strategies and unmet needs.
- **Education**: classroom observations and teacher interviews, coded by thematic analysis to identify inclusive teaching practices.
- **UX research**: user interviews about an onboarding flow, analysed by content analysis to surface friction points and unmet expectations.
- **Political science**: speeches by policymakers on climate adaptation, examined by discourse analysis to map how responsibility is framed.
- **Sociology**: biographical interviews with migrants, coded through grounded theory to build a substantive theory of integration trajectories.
- **Journalism**: editorial documents and reporter interviews, coded to understand how a publication shifted its audience strategy.
