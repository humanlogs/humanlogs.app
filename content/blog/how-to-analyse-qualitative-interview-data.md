You have finished the fieldwork. Ten, twenty, maybe forty interviews are recorded, and a stack of transcripts is sitting in a folder. Now comes the part that separates a solid qualitative study from a weak one: turning a pile of conversations into findings that hold up under scrutiny. Learning how to analyse qualitative interview data is less about picking the right software and more about a disciplined process, one that respects how much context lives in a spoken conversation that a survey response or a document simply does not have.

This guide walks through that process end to end: what makes interview data distinct, a step-by-step method, how to choose an analytical approach, and the mistakes that most often undermine an otherwise good interview study.

## What makes interview data different to analyse?

Interviews are dialogic. A participant's answer is shaped by the question that preceded it, by follow-up probes, by hesitations and by what the interviewer did or did not pursue. That has real consequences for analysis:

- **Meaning is distributed across the conversation.** A single quote pulled out of context can misrepresent what a participant meant. You often need the surrounding exchange, including the interviewer's question, to code a passage correctly.
- **Depth over volume.** Compared to survey responses, you usually have far fewer participants but much more material per participant, so the analytical unit shifts from "how many people said X" to "how did this person construct their account, and where does it converge with or diverge from others."
- **The interviewer is part of the data.** Rapport, probing style and even the interviewer's identity can shape what participants say. Reflexive notes about the interview itself, not just its content, are part of a rigorous analysis.
- **Non-verbal and paralinguistic cues matter.** Pauses, laughter, tone shifts and self-corrections often carry as much analytical weight as the words themselves, which is why verbatim transcription (not a cleaned-up summary) is the starting point.

## How do you analyse qualitative interview data? A step-by-step process

### 1. Transcribe verbatim, then read before you code

Before any coding happens, every interview needs an accurate, verbatim transcript, hesitations, false starts and filler words included, not silently tidied up. Read the full corpus once, start to finish, without coding. This immersion phase is where you start noticing recurring language and tentative patterns, and it prevents you from imposing categories too early.

### 2. Code the first few interviews closely

Start with in-vivo or descriptive codes on the first three to five transcripts: short labels attached to meaningful segments, in the participant's own words where possible. Keep a running codebook with a definition and an example quote for every code you create, this is what lets you (or a second coder) apply codes consistently later.

### 3. Compare across interviews, not just within one

This is the step that is easy to skip and that most distinguishes interview analysis from analysing a single document. As you code additional transcripts, actively look for where participants agree, where they diverge, and where a code from one interview simply does not appear in another. Constant comparison, going back and forth between transcripts rather than coding them once in sequence, is what surfaces genuine patterns instead of coincidences.

### 4. Group codes into categories, then themes

Cluster related codes into broader categories, then into themes that answer your research question. A theme should be grounded in multiple interviews (not necessarily all of them) and should represent a genuine pattern of meaning, not just a topic that came up.

### 5. Check for saturation

Keep coding new interviews until they stop producing new codes and start reinforcing existing ones. That point, not an arbitrary sample size decided in advance, is the real signal that you have enough interviews. If you are still finding new codes in your last three transcripts, your corpus is not saturated yet.

### 6. Return to the audio, not just the transcript

Before finalising a theme built on a striking quote, listen back to the original recording. Tone, timing and emphasis are frequently lost even in a careful verbatim transcript, and they can change how a passage should be interpreted.

### 7. Write with evidence, not just assertion

Present each theme with two or three representative verbatim extracts, ideally from different participants, and explain explicitly how the extract supports the claim. A reader should be able to trace your interpretation back to the data.

## Which method fits interview data best?

**Thematic analysis** is the most common default: flexible, well documented (Braun and Clarke's six-phase framework is the standard reference), and works across most theoretical positions.

**Grounded theory** suits interview data particularly well when your goal is to build an explanatory theory rather than describe a phenomenon, because it is designed around iterative rounds of interviewing and coding until theoretical saturation.

**Interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA)** is built for interview data specifically: a small sample (often six to ten participants), analysed one transcript at a time in depth before cross-case themes are drawn out. It is a poor fit if you have thirty interviews and limited time, but a strong one for a focused study of lived experience.

**Content analysis** is a reasonable choice when you need to compare interviews against a more structured, deductive coding scheme, for instance when your interview guide already maps closely onto your research questions.

## Common mistakes when analysing qualitative interview data

- **Coding before finishing all interviews.** Locking in a coding scheme too early biases you toward finding what you already coded for in the interviews you have not analysed yet.
- **Treating a transcript as if it stands alone.** Interview meaning is co-constructed. Ignoring the interviewer's question, or the flow of the conversation, when interpreting an answer is a common source of misreading.
- **Building a theme from a single striking quote.** A memorable line is not automatically a theme. Check that the underlying pattern recurs across the corpus before you report it as a finding.
- **Skipping reflexivity.** Not documenting your own position, assumptions and role as interviewer makes it harder for readers, and for you, to assess how those factors shaped the analysis.
- **Working from an imprecise transcript.** Missed words, misattributed speakers in multi-participant interviews, or hesitations edited out for readability all quietly distort the coding that follows.

## How many interviews do you need before you can start coding?

You do not need to wait for the whole dataset. Start coding as soon as you have three to five transcripts, so you can catch problems with your coding scheme or your interview guide early, while there is still time to adjust. Formal cross-case comparison and saturation checks come later, once a meaningful share of the corpus is in.
