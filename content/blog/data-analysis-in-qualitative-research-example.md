Most methods chapters explain thematic analysis, grounded theory, or content analysis in the abstract: code the data, group the codes, find the themes. What they rarely show is what that actually looks like on a real page of transcript. If you already know the theory and just want to see data analysis in qualitative research example form, start to finish, this walks through one.

The example below uses thematic analysis, the most common approach, on a short interview excerpt. The steps generalise to most other qualitative methods with minor adjustments.

## The research question and the excerpt

Say the study asks: *how do remote employees experience feeling included in their team?* Below is a short excerpt from one interview (composite, anonymised, adapted from patterns common in remote-work research).

> **Interviewer:** Can you tell me about a time you felt left out at work, even remotely?
>
> **Participant:** Yeah, there was this stretch where every decision seemed to get made in a Slack thread I wasn't in. Not on purpose, I don't think. People just message whoever's around. But I'd log on Monday and there'd be a whole new plan and I'd missed the conversation. I started scheduling extra one-on-ones just to catch up, which, I mean, that's not really inclusion, that's me chasing information.

That single paragraph is enough to demonstrate the full process.

## Step 1: Familiarisation

Before coding anything, read the excerpt (and the full transcript, in a real project) without trying to categorise it. Note first impressions: the participant frames exclusion as unintentional ("not on purpose"), locates it in a specific tool (Slack), and describes compensating for it through extra effort (scheduling one-on-ones). Those three observations already hint at where the coding will go, but nothing gets labelled yet. Skipping this step and jumping straight to codes is the most common way researchers miss a theme that only becomes visible once you've read the whole account.

## Step 2: Line-by-line coding

Coding means attaching a short label to each meaningful segment. Codes can be close to the participant's own words (in-vivo) or more interpretive.

| Excerpt segment | Code |
| --- | --- |
| "every decision seemed to get made in a Slack thread I wasn't in" | Exclusion from informal decision-making |
| "not on purpose, I don't think" | Attributing exclusion to structure, not intent |
| "people just message whoever's around" | Proximity bias in remote communication |
| "I'd missed the conversation" | Information lag |
| "I started scheduling extra one-on-ones" | Self-initiated compensation |
| "that's not really inclusion, that's me chasing information" | Participant's own critique of the workaround |

Six codes from four sentences. This is normal, real transcripts generate far more codes than most first-time coders expect, and pruning happens later, not now.

## Step 3: Grouping codes into themes

With codes for one participant done (and normally repeated across the full sample), the next step looks for patterns across codes. Here, three of the six codes cluster around *how exclusion happens* (informal decision-making, proximity bias, information lag), and two cluster around *how the participant responds to it* (self-initiated compensation, and awareness that the response isn't a real fix). That split becomes two candidate themes:

- **Theme A: Inclusion breaks down informally, not through decisions.** Nobody decided to exclude the participant; the exclusion is a byproduct of who happens to be online when things get discussed.
- **Theme B: The burden of staying included falls on the excluded person.** The participant, not the team, does the extra work to stay in the loop, and recognises that this isn't the same as being included.

A theme needs to recur across multiple participants, not just this one excerpt, before it earns a place in the final analysis. In a real dataset, you would check both candidate themes against every other transcript at this point.

## Step 4: From themes to a written finding

The last step turns themes into an argument, anchored in evidence, not a list.

> *Participants did not describe exclusion as a deliberate act. Instead, they located it in the informal, tool-mediated flow of daily work: decisions surfaced in whichever channel happened to be active, and being absent from that channel at the right moment was enough to miss a decision entirely (Theme A). Notably, the responsibility for closing that gap fell on the excluded employee, who described compensating through extra effort ("I started scheduling extra one-on-ones") while explicitly naming that effort as inadequate ("that's not really inclusion, that's me chasing information") (Theme B). This suggests remote inclusion breaks down at the level of communication infrastructure, not intent, and that current workarounds shift the burden onto the person already excluded.*

That paragraph is the actual output of qualitative analysis: not the codes, not the themes, but a claim, grounded in the participant's words, that answers the research question.

## Common mistakes this example avoids

**Coding too early.** Labelling segments before finishing familiarisation locks in categories that don't fit the fuller picture.

**Treating one striking quote as a theme.** A theme needs to recur. One vivid excerpt is evidence for a theme, not a theme by itself.

**Skipping the participant's own interpretation.** The line "that's not really inclusion, that's me chasing information" is the participant analysing their own experience. Missing it would have flattened Theme B into a simple coping-strategy code instead of a finding about where responsibility sits.

**Writing up themes as a list instead of an argument.** "Theme A: exclusion. Theme B: compensation." tells the reader nothing about how they relate. The written finding above connects them causally, which is what reviewers and readers are actually looking for.
